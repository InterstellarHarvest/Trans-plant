PART 1 · HOW TO PLAY

Everything a captain needs to know, with nothing spoiled that the trail should surprise you with.

The idea

Earth is dying. You have a ship, a crop, and a plan. The plan is not good.

You are the captain of a colony ship carrying one living crop to a new world. Your job is to get that plant across a freshly generated star map and into new soil, alive. The crop is the mission: you can arrive with a full crew, a shiny hull and a pile of gold, but if the plant in the growbay is dead, the colony has nothing to grow.

Between you and the destination are stations, planets, asteroid fields, nebulae, derelict ships, anomalies and a great deal of nothing. Every stop brings an event and a choice. Every choice costs something: days, supplies, morale, hull, gold. Your supplies drain as you fly, your crew gets tired and hurt, and the colony would like you to arrive before the deadline.

There are three trails, and you pick one at the start of every run:

TrailLengthWhat it is like
LunarAbout three months, around a dozen stops, one forkThe gentle one. Established corridors and plenty of stations. A good first run.
MarsAbout six months, around nineteen stops, two forksYou leave short on water and food, and the trail makes you work for every drop.
InterstellarAbout ten months, around two dozen stops, three forksThe long haul to Far Garden. Stranger places, long empty stretches, and the hardest trouble saved for the end.

A run is a roguelike voyage: one life, no going back along the route, and a new map, new events and a new ending each time.

Your first run

This walkthrough follows a real Lunar run from the title screen to the colony. Your run will meet different events, but every run follows the same shape.

1 · The title screen

Press any key (or click) to skip the opening lines. NEW GAME starts the setup wizard. CONTINUE picks up your autosaved voyage, if you have one. RECORDS lights up once you have finished a run and shows your past voyages and best scores.

2 · The setup wizard

Six quick steps, shown as pips across the top. Pick a card on the right and its flavor line appears on the left. NEXT (or Enter) moves on, BACK goes back, and on the first step MENU returns to the title. Each card states what it does in its green stat line, and those numbers are the game's real ones.

  • Trail. Lunar, Mars or Interstellar (see The idea). Longer trails mean more stops, more forks, tighter supplies and harder events.
  • AI. The ship computer narrates the whole voyage, comments on everything you do, and gives one passive perk. See The five AIs.
  • Captain. That is you. Your background is what you studied, and it gives you a half-strength version of one specialist's skill, sometimes with a starting item or extra gold. See Captains.
  • Crew. Pick up to two of seven specialists. Each one has an always-on passive skill, an active skill you trigger yourself, and choices in events that only they can unlock. Picking fewer than two asks you to confirm; picking none means flying solo. See Crew roles.
  • Crop. What you are keeping alive: wheat, tomatoes, sweet potatoes, soybeans or zinnias. They differ in how fast they grow, how thirsty they are, what they resist and what a harvest gives. See Crops.
  • Ship name. The ship arrives with a name from the colony registry. RE-ROLL draws another, or type your own. Then LAUNCH.

A good first run: the Lunar trail, any AI that amuses you, and a botanist or an engineer in the crew. Sweet potatoes or wheat forgive mistakes. Tomatoes and zinnias are for when you want drama.

3 · Departure, at the station

After LAUNCH the voyage starts on day 0 at Earth Orbital Station, and something happens before you have even left: the departure event. Events are the heart of the game. A scene and a story panel fill the screen, the text types itself out, and once it has landed your choices appear on the right. Click anywhere on the story to make the text finish at once.

The five dots at the top right are a compact gauge you see during events: fuel, food, oxygen, hull and the crew's average morale. The line at the top left is your AI's running commentary. We will make this choice in step 6.

4 · The cruise hub

When the departure is over, you land on the cruise hub, the screen you come back to between every stop. The ship is still parked at the station, so the hub opens stopped: the Stop menu sits in the right-hand column, and the AI says its piece in a speech bubble over the ship.

Everything on this screen is explained in The screens, one by one. For now, the key parts are:

  • the header: ship name, a news ticker, your gold, the cargo hold and map buttons, and the day counter with the days left before the deadline;
  • the standing orders strip under the ship (engines, rations, water) which opens ORDERS;
  • the crew strip, the crop card and the five supply bars along the bottom;
  • the big STOP / RESUME button in the bottom right corner.

Your AI also gives first-run tips like the one in the bubble, one per stop, the first time each topic comes up. You can switch them off in the pause menu's SETTINGS.

5 · Stopping: the Stop menu

Every time you arrive somewhere and finish its event, the ship is stopped there and the Stop menu opens. Its jobs depend on the kind of place: at a habitable planet you can GATHER food, SCAN ahead, TRADE if a trader is in range, REST the crew or run the FABRICATOR.

Every job costs time, and most cost a little morale (the effort tax, see Events and choices). You can do as many jobs as you like at a stop, one after another. When you are done, press RESUME to fly on. Doing nothing is also a choice: the deadline is always ticking.

6 · An event, and making a choice

Back to that departure. Hover over a choice to see what it really costs: the days it takes and any supplies it spends. Here, signing up a breakfast-cereal sponsor takes a day and costs a little morale.

Click a choice (or press its number key, 1 to 9) and the outcome plays. What it did to the ship is summed up in the AI log and the Ship's Log: in this run, "+12 GOLD, −2 MORALE, 1 DAY". Some choices lead to a second set of choices before the outcome; some start a hands-on minigame or a ship-to-ship fight.

7 · Traveling on, and forks

Press RESUME. The ship flies the leg to the next stop: the stars streak past at the speed your ENGINES order sets, the days of the leg pass, and fuel, food, water and oxygen are drawn down. You arrive, the stop's event plays, and you are stopped again. That is the rhythm of the whole voyage.

The route is a line of stops with a few forks. At a fork, an event asks you to pick a lane, and you cannot come back for the other one. The map button in the header shows the whole route; lanes you have not chosen yet are marked with question marks.

A good SCAN just before a fork reports what waits at the first stop down each lane.

8 · A station visit

Stations are where you shop, heal, repair and recharge. Arriving at one opens the approach on the bridge with the station in the window. Sometimes the dockmaster calls first; otherwise HAIL opens comms. Then DOCK. Some stations charge a small parking fee, and DOCK says so.

Once docked, your crew's active skills recharge. Market Row is always there; sometimes there is also someone in another room (a cantina, a clinic, an office) with something to say. Each room plays once per visit. When you LEAVE STATION you are back on the hub, stopped at the station, and the Stop menu now includes the station services you can pay for: MEDBAY, DRYDOCK and FAB SERVICE (each only shown when you need it), plus TRADE to go back to the market.

9 · Arriving

Reach the last stop and the voyage is over. The arrival screen shows the colony with your crop, under a title that tells you how well it went. VIEW REPORT opens the report card.

The report card shows:

  • the tier title, an arrival line about your crop at its new home, and a closing quote;
  • the Run Report: how many of the people who launched are still aboard, how full your fuel, food, water, hull and morale were, the day you arrived against the deadline (days early, on time, or days late), and your score, with NEW BEST when it beats your record for that destination;
  • score extras, when you earned them (soil surveys, a crop bonus);
  • READ THE COLONY REPORT, which unfolds the full ending text and the Ship's Record, the most notable things you did on the way;
  • NEW RUN, and back to the title.

A run that ends early (see How a run can end) gets its own screen with an epitaph and the Ship's Record instead.

The AI's speech bubble

While you are stopped the Stop menu covers the log column, so your AI talks to you in a speech bubble over the ship instead: its portrait and its newest three lines for this stop (tips, comments, warnings, memories of earlier choices). Click the bubble or its × to dismiss it; RESUME clears it too. Every line is also kept in the log and the Ship's Log, so nothing is lost. You can see the bubble in the walkthrough's hub screenshot.

The Stop menu

The Stop menu lists the jobs this place allows. Its header names the kind of place (DEEP SPACE, ASTEROID FIELD, NEBULA, STATION APPROACH…) with a line of mood, and a LOG button that opens the Ship's Log. Each job row shows its effort pips, how long it takes, and a one-line description. Hover for the details, including the morale the job will cost each crew member. A locked job says what it needs. Jobs with nothing to work on (REPAIR with a full hull, MEDBAY with nobody hurt) are not listed at all.

Events and choices

Every stop has one event. Some are quick decisions, some have several rounds of choices, some put you face to face with someone on a screen or in a corridor, and some turn into minigames or fights. The choices column is where the decisions are made, and it tells you a lot if you know how to read it.

  • The sub-label is the small line under a choice. It is always one line, and the full story is in the tooltip. It may say what the choice needs, preview what it does, or describe the job.
  • Effort pips ●●○○○ show how much work a choice is, from one to five. Every pip costs morale: each crew member pays the effort tax when you pick it, and more pips cost more morale, climbing faster than the pips do. The cost is in the tooltip, on Stop-menu jobs and event choices alike. With no crew aboard there is nobody to tax.
  • Effort checks. Hands-on jobs show Effort 65 / 40 · 2 days · 90% clean: the effort you can bring against the effort the job needs, the time it takes, and the chance it goes cleanly. Your own hands, every crew member, the right specialist and the right tool all add effort. A one-use tool (a repair kit, say) is spent when it counts. Short of effort, the job still gets done, just worse: the tooltip spells out exactly what "worse" costs. Even a well-equipped crew is never certain.
  • Locked choices show a padlock and Need: …: a crew role, an item, an amount of gold, or several at once. A crew member who is too badly hurt cannot help, and the lock says so. Some choices stay hidden entirely until something earlier in the voyage opens them.
  • Gate icons. An unlocked choice that uses something you have shows its icon: the crew member's portrait, the item, the gold.
  • Previews. Some choices show what they will do before you pick them. Your AI might (MARV flags the most expensive option), a clean SCAN does, some items do, and an Academic captain gets one research preview per station leg.
  • Risky choices have red sub-labels: they can go badly.
  • Tooltips. Hover any choice for the full picture: the days it takes, everything it costs, the effort and its morale tax, the odds, and what happens if you fall short.
  • Leaving. Many layered events have a red leave row at the bottom (LEAVE, NOT NOW, BACK TO DOCK…). It ends the scene with no outcome, unless its own sub-label or tooltip says otherwise: some leave rows are an exit with a price (backing off from a fight is never free). A few emergencies cannot be left, and their leave row is locked.

Many choices also take time. A quick decision is a day; real work can be several days; a few big undertakings take a couple of weeks. The tooltip always says.

ORDERS

Your standing orders apply every day until you change them. Click the orders strip under the ship (or a readout in it) to open ORDERS. Each row shows a projection that updates as you try options; APPLY keeps the change, CANCEL throws it away.

  • ENGINES sets the pace. LIGHT SPEED: crawl, burn little, the crew rests. RIDICULOUS: the default. LUDICROUS: faster, burns harder, the crew tires. PLAID: fastest, bleeds fuel and crew, and running at Plaid leg after leg heats the drive. The row projects your ARRIVAL day against the deadline, the day the fuel would run out, and the morale trend.
  • RATIONS: VENDING MACHINE (half rations: food lasts, morale sinks), STANDARD (morale holds), PIG OUT (extra: morale lifts, food empties fast). The row shows food per day and the day the food runs out.
  • WATER ALLOCATION splits the ship's water between the plant and the crew. Drag the slider. The marker shows what the crop needs; below it the crop is thirsty and slows, too far above it risks root rot, and starving the crew's side hurts morale and eventually health. The row projects when the plant matures and how the crew is doing.

The ARRIVAL projection is the same one the game uses to decide you are behind schedule. If it shows a ⚠ and a day past the deadline, you are on course to arrive late.

The cargo hold

The crate button in the header opens the hold. The top shows your fuel, food and water tanks with their level, daily use and the day each would run empty. Below are three tabs: GEAR (instruments, consumables, trade goods), MATERIALS and UPGRADES, with a SORT menu. The header counts slots used against capacity.

From the detail panel you can USE a consumable (a fuel cell, a ration brick, a crop treatment…), SELL it to the market while you are docked (no day spent), or JETTISON it to free a slot. Upgrades work just by being aboard.

The crew dossier

Click a crew card for their dossier: portrait, health and morale, a short bio, any status effects (wounded, morale crisis, deserting, going hungry or thirsty…), their PASSIVE skill (always on) and their ACTIVE skill with its uses and a USE button. Active skills recharge when you dock at a station. Arrows flip between crew members, and the captain has a shorter dossier of their own.

The growbay

Click the crop card to visit your crop. The growbay shows growth and health bars, the plant itself at its current stage, the stage name, the day the harvest will be ready, who is tending the plant, and every growth modifier in play. Under MONITORED are the threats that are not happening right now.

  • Water. Set the plant's share of the water. The plant's need is marked on the track.
  • Tender. Who looks after the plant. A botanist tends by default and is by far the best at it; anyone else helps a little. Click the tender card to change.
  • TREAT clears a pest outbreak. It takes a day of work unless a botanist handles it; the button's tooltip says which.
  • INSPECT opens the full read: vital signs, growth rate, species notes, the four stages and what can go wrong in each, and every active threat with its cause and fix.
  • HARVEST unlocks when growth reaches 100%. It gives food (zinnias give morale instead) and the plant starts a fresh cycle. Food that will not fit in the stores is pressed into ration bricks if the hold has room.

The fabricator

The fabricator turns materials into useful things. Open it from the Stop menu. Pick a recipe on the left to see what it makes, what it needs (with what you have), how many days it takes, how much wear it adds, the chance of a misfire and its morale cost. CRAFT starts the job.

  • Crafting takes real days, one at a time, while the fabricator is open, and the ship's usual daily drain applies. Close it and the job is held until you come back.
  • CANCEL before the first day finishes gives everything back; after that you get half the materials back.
  • Wear builds with every print and makes misfires more likely. At full wear the fabricator breaks. An engineer reduces wear, and a station's FAB SERVICE resets it.

The market and Market Row

At a station, TRADE (or the MARKET room when docked) takes you to Market Row, where a vendor runs a stall; out on the trail, TRADE hails a passing trader ship. BROWSE THE WARES opens the market.

  • BUY rows sell an item for gold. Station markets stock a wide catalog, including ship upgrades; a trader ship carries a smaller hold.
  • TRADE rows are barter: they take one of your goods instead of gold.
  • SELL pays gold for your items and materials. Trade goods each have their own value; a spectrometer aboard tells you exactly what it is before you sell.
  • Discounts (CHIP, a Merchant captain) apply to what you buy, never to what you sell.
  • STEP BACK closes the market. Browsing is free; leaving after you bought or sold costs a day.

The route map

The map button in the header shows the whole route: every stop, colored by type, from Departure to your destination, with YOU ARE HERE over the ship. Stops you have passed are marked visited. Click a stop to read what it is. Forks split into named lanes; until you pick, the lanes beyond them read ???. The map is for looking only: you choose lanes in the fork's event.

The Ship's Log

The LOG button in the Stop menu (or SHIP'S LOG in the pause terminal) opens the voyage's journal, written by your AI. It records every event you met and what you chose, what it really did to the ship, every arrival and what the leg cost, crop milestones, fights, and people joining or leaving. Entries with a link icon point to the real science behind a joke. Anyone lost on the voyage gets a memorial page.

The pause terminal

Press Esc any time for the Bridge Terminal. Time stops while it is open. It opens on a short ship status report.

  • RESUME closes the terminal.
  • SETTINGS: cursor color and outline, SHOW TIPS (the AI's first-run tips), and TEXT SPEED for the typewriter text.
  • SAVE and LOAD: three local save slots. You can save between encounters (not in the middle of an event or a job), and a loaded game returns to the hub. The game also autosaves as you play; CONTINUE on the title screen resumes it.
  • SHIP'S LOG opens the journal.
  • HELP lists the controls: click to act, hover to preview, drag sliders, Esc for this terminal or to close a window, 1–9 to pick a choice or a Stop-menu job, Tab and Enter to move between buttons and press one.
  • QUIT returns to the title screen after a confirmation. Anything since the last save or autosave is lost.

Your saves live in your browser, on this computer. Clearing the browser's site data clears them too.

Records

RECORDS on the title screen lists your best arrival per destination (score, tier, ship, crop, captain, day and crew) and your last 10 voyages, newest first: arrivals with their tier and score, and runs that ended early with how they ended. Only arrivals whose crop made it alive count as a best. CLEAR RECORDS wipes them, after asking.

What everything means

The seven resources

Fuel, food, water, oxygen and hull are ship supplies, each out of 100 (two upgrades raise the fuel and hull caps). Morale belongs to each crew member. Gold is money. The supply bars turn to warning colors when a supply runs low, and your AI will mention it.

HP, wounds and morale

  • Health (HP) runs from 100 down. Below 40 a person is wounded: their skills work at half strength and their HP bar turns amber. At 25 or below it turns red, and they are too hurt to help with choices that need them. At zero they die. The captain has real health too.
  • Healing: everyone recovers a little each leg (faster with a medic aboard). The medic's Treatment, a medkit, and MEDBAY at a station heal properly.
  • Morale is per person. Changes to "the crew" hit everyone, but each specialist also reacts to their own concern: the botanist to the crop's health, the engineer to hull damage (and cheers up at a finished repair), the chef to short rations, the medic to injuries and deaths, the pilot to the pace, the xenobiologist to alien finds, the diplomat to fighting, and everyone to their own wounds. Hover a morale bar to see why it moved. With only one crew member aboard, those personal concerns count half.
  • Desertion. When someone's morale hits zero you are warned, and they will walk off at the next station unless their morale is back up to 25 by then. The morale bar and dossier say so.
  • Mutiny. If the crew's average morale reaches zero, they take the ship, and the run ends.
  • The captain has no morale. Flying solo (no crew at all) means no morale to manage and no effort tax, but also no specialists and fewer hands.
  • A vacant crew slot can sometimes be filled on the way, by someone you take aboard. The ship never carries more than captain plus two crew; anyone extra rides along as a passenger.

Crops and how they grow

Your crop grows from 0% to 100% through four stages: Sprouting, Vegetative, Flowering and Maturing. At 100% you can harvest it, and it starts again from a fresh planting. Its health is what matters for the mission: it must be above zero when you arrive.

  • Growth is fastest when the plant gets the water it needs and someone good tends it. Being thirsty, pests, and the wrong surroundings slow it down.
  • Health slowly recovers with good watering and falls with thirst, pests and stress. Ordinary imperfect care mostly stalls it; real neglect kills.
  • Surroundings matter. Deep void runs cold, anomalies run hot, nebulae and the void are dim. The growbay shows these as modifiers while you are there; they pass when you move on.
  • Pests turn up now and then once the plant is established. TREAT them in the growbay or use a crop treatment.
  • A healthy crop helps the air: it makes some of the oxygen the crew breathes.

Items and materials

  • Items are things: instruments (a gas analyzer, a geiger counter, a portable drill…), consumables (fuel cells, ration bricks, water and O2 canisters, medkits, hull patches, crop treatments), trade goods worth gold, curiosities, and upgrades (a bigger fuel tank, hull plating, a shield module…). Gear takes a cargo slot each; small consumables stack several to a slot; upgrades take no slot and work as long as they are aboard. Many items open choices in events; the hold's detail panel lists where an item is used.
  • Materials are raw stock: metal, scrap, minerals, exotic matter and biocomponents. They come from mining, salvage, fights and events, and go into REPAIR and the fabricator, or get sold. Every 10 of a material takes one cargo slot.
  • The hold has limits. Capacity depends on the trail (and one upgrade adds more). A full hold means new finds stay behind: briefings warn you before you spend the time.

The minigames

Hands-on jobs open as minigames. Each starts on a briefing with your AI's take and the rules, then BEGIN. The result is graded EXCELLENT, ADEQUATE or INSUFFICIENT, and a better grade means a better reward. When an event launches a minigame, the briefing lists exactly what each grade earns.

Deadlines and arriving late

Each trail has a deadline: the day the colony expects you. The day counter shows the days left, and ORDERS projects your arrival day at the pace you have set. If that projection drifts past the deadline, you are behind schedule, and the trail may let you know.

Being late never ends a run. When the deadline passes, your AI says so once and the ship flies on. You still arrive and still get your ending; the day counter switches to counting the days late, the report says how many, and every late day comes off your score. Arriving early adds to it.

How a run can end

Arrival. Reach the destination and the run is scored. The score weighs how much of your crew made it, how well-stocked the ship is (fuel, food, water, hull and morale), and how early or late you are, plus any extras such as soil surveys and a crop bonus. The score sorts the arrival into one of four tiers, from a triumphant MADE IT down to an arrival nobody is quite sure about, each with its own endings to discover. Each trail has its own scale, so a good Interstellar arrival is judged against the Interstellar trail. And if the crop is dead when you arrive, none of that matters: the crew made it, the mission did not, and the game will let you sit with that.

Ending early. A run can also end on the way:

  • the fuel runs out and the ship is adrift;
  • the hull fails;
  • the captain dies (whether or not anyone else is left aboard);
  • the crew's average morale hits zero and they mutiny;
  • too many days with no food, no water or no oxygen;
  • the crop dies.

Some of these leave a door open. When the crop dies you can choose to keep flying, though your arrival can then only be the hollow kind. And a certain item can give a stranded ship one last chance. If the whole crew is lost but the captain lives, the captain flies on alone.

Tips for surviving

Every trail

  • The crop comes first. Check the crop card every stop. Keep its water share at or just above what it needs, TREAT pests promptly, and harvest when it is ready: the food (or the morale) is yours, and the plant starts again.
  • Read the tooltips. Every cost the game knows about is on a tooltip before you commit: days, supplies, morale, odds, and what happens if you fall short.
  • Mind the pips. Heavy jobs tax every crew member's morale. A few big jobs in a row can leave the crew miserable even when everything went well.
  • Dock at stations. Docking recharges active skills, stations have the biggest market, and only stations will heal your crew or patch your hull for gold. Waving off is free in time but expensive in options.
  • Salvage pays. Derelicts and wreckage are where steady gold and materials come from, and gold buys medbays, drydocks and upgrades.
  • Use your specialists' active skills. They recharge at the next station, so an unused one is wasted.
  • Watch the ORDERS projection. It tells you the day you will arrive and the day each supply runs out at your current settings.
  • SCAN before a fork when you can spare the day.
  • Do not burn Plaid forever. Fast is tempting; the engines, the fuel and the crew all pay for it.

Lunar: the gentle trail

  • Lunar forgives a lot. Use it to learn the screens, try the minigames and get to know a crew.
  • Stations are plentiful, so spend gold freely on repairs and healing.
  • With a short trail, every lost day is a bigger share of the deadline: do not rest more than you need to.

Mars: the thirsty trail

  • You leave short on water and food, and events here ask much more of both. Water is the supply most likely to bite.
  • Do not over-water the crop "to be safe": give it what it needs and keep the rest for the crew. Ice from mining asteroids and water canisters are worth the time.
  • A chef, sensible rations and regular harvests keep the larder going; a food crop pays for itself here.
  • Trouble gets worse as the trail goes on. Reach the back half of the trip with supplies in hand, not scraping by.

Interstellar: the long trail

  • It is a long way through stranger places: voids, anomalies, long stretches between stations. Plan for long gaps between resupplies.
  • The crew starts less cheerful. Keep morale healthy early; it is much harder to rebuild later.
  • The hardest events wait for the final stretch. Bank fuel, hull and spare consumables for the end.
  • The fabricator earns its keep here: keep some materials and service it at stations.
  • Fuel matters over this distance. A pilot, sensible engine settings and a stock of fuel cells keep you from drifting.
