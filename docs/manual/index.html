PART 1 · HOW TO PLAY

Everything a captain needs to know, with nothing spoiled that the trail should surprise you with.

The idea

Earth is dying. You have a ship, a crop, and a plan. The plan is not good.

You are the captain of a colony ship carrying one living crop to a new world. Your job is to get that plant across a freshly generated star map and into new soil, alive. The crop is the mission: you can arrive with a full crew, a shiny hull and a pile of gold, but if the plant in the growbay is dead, the colony has nothing to grow.

Between you and the destination are stations, planets, asteroid fields, nebulae, derelict ships, anomalies and a great deal of nothing. Every stop brings an event and a choice. Every choice costs something: days, supplies, morale, hull, gold. Your supplies drain as you fly, your crew gets tired and hurt, and the colony would like you to arrive before the deadline.

There are three trails, and you pick one at the start of every run:

TrailLengthWhat it is like
LunarAbout three months, around a dozen stops, one forkThe gentle one. Established corridors and plenty of stations. A good first run.
MarsAbout six months, around nineteen stops, two forksYou leave short on water and food, and the trail makes you work for every drop.
InterstellarAbout ten months, around two dozen stops, three forksThe long haul to Far Garden. Stranger places, long empty stretches, and the hardest trouble saved for the end.

A run is a roguelike voyage: one life, no going back along the route, and a new map, new events and a new ending each time.

Your first run

This walkthrough follows a real Lunar run from the title screen to the colony. Your run will meet different events, but every run follows the same shape.

1 · The title screen

The menu lives on the terminal of the ship's observation deck. Press any key (or click) to skip the opening lines. NEW GAME starts the setup wizard. CONTINUE picks up your autosaved voyage, if you have one. RECORDS lights up once you have finished a run and shows your past voyages and best scores. A button's lamp is lit when it can do something, and the SHIP STATUS screen below says what is aboard, or what the button you point at does. NEW GAME and CONTINUE play a short departure first; any key or click skips it.

2 · The setup wizard

Six quick steps, shown as pips across the top. Pick a card on the right and its flavor line appears on the left. NEXT (or Enter) moves on, BACK goes back, and on the first step MENU returns to the title. Each card states what it does in its green stat line, and those numbers are the game's real ones.

  • Trail. Lunar, Mars or Interstellar (see The idea). Longer trails mean more stops, more forks, tighter supplies and harder events.
  • AI. The ship computer narrates the whole voyage, comments on everything you do, and gives one passive perk. See The five AIs.
  • Captain. That is you. Your background is what you studied, and it gives you a half-strength version of one specialist's skill, sometimes with a starting item or extra gold. See Captains.
  • Crew. Pick up to two of seven specialists. Each one has an always-on passive skill, an active skill you trigger yourself, and choices in events that only they can unlock. Picking fewer than two asks you to confirm; picking none means flying solo. See Crew roles.
  • Crop. What you are keeping alive: wheat, tomatoes, sweet potatoes, soybeans or zinnias. They differ in how fast they grow, how thirsty they are, what they resist and what a harvest gives. See Crops.
  • Ship name. The ship arrives with a name from the colony registry. RE-ROLL draws another, or type your own. Then LAUNCH.

A good first run: the Lunar trail, any AI that amuses you, and a botanist or an engineer in the crew. Sweet potatoes or wheat forgive mistakes. Tomatoes and zinnias are for when you want drama.

3 · Departure, at the station

After LAUNCH the voyage starts on day 0 at Earth Orbital Station, and something happens before you have even left: the departure event. Events are the heart of the game. A scene and a story panel fill the screen, the text types itself out, and once it has landed your choices appear on the right. Click anywhere on the story to make the text finish at once.

The five dots at the top right are a compact gauge you see during events: fuel, food, oxygen, hull and the crew's average morale. The line at the top left is your AI's running commentary. We will make this choice in step 6.

4 · The cruise hub

When the departure is over, you land on the cruise hub, the screen you come back to between every stop. The ship is still parked at the station, so the hub opens stopped: the Stop menu sits in the right-hand column, and the AI says its piece in a speech bubble over the ship.

Everything on this screen is explained in The screens, one by one. For now, the key parts are:

  • the header: ship name, a news ticker, your gold, the cargo hold and map buttons, and the day counter with the days left before the deadline;
  • the standing orders strip under the ship (engines, rations, water) which opens ORDERS;
  • the crew strip, the crop card and the five supply bars along the bottom;
  • the big STOP / RESUME button in the bottom right corner.

Your AI also gives first-run tips like the one in the bubble, one per stop, the first time each topic comes up. You can switch them off in the pause menu's SETTINGS.

5 · Stopping: the Stop menu

Every time you arrive somewhere and finish its event, the ship is stopped there and the Stop menu opens. Its jobs depend on the kind of place: at a habitable planet you can GATHER food, SCAN ahead, TRADE if a trader is in range, REST the crew or run the FABRICATOR.

Every job costs time, and most cost a little morale (the effort tax, see Events and choices). You can do as many jobs as you like at a stop, one after another. When you are done, press RESUME to fly on. Doing nothing is also a choice: the deadline is always ticking.

6 · An event, and making a choice

Back to that departure. Hover over a choice to see what it really costs: the days it takes and any supplies it spends. Here, signing up a breakfast-cereal sponsor takes a day and costs a little morale.

Click a choice (or press its number key, 1 to 9) and the outcome plays. What it did to the ship is summed up in the AI log and the Ship's Log: in this run, "+12 GOLD, −2 MORALE, 1 DAY". Some choices lead to a second set of choices before the outcome; some start a hands-on minigame or a ship-to-ship fight.

7 · Traveling on, and forks

Press RESUME. The ship flies the leg to the next stop: the stars streak past at the speed your ENGINES order sets, the days of the leg pass, and fuel, food, water and oxygen are drawn down. You arrive, the stop's event plays, and you are stopped again. That is the rhythm of the whole voyage.

The route is a line of stops with a few forks. At a fork, an event asks you to pick a lane, and you cannot come back for the other one. The map button in the header shows the whole route; lanes you have not chosen yet are marked with question marks.

A good SCAN just before a fork reports what waits at the first stop down each lane.

8 · A station visit

Stations are where you shop, heal, repair and recharge. Arriving at one opens the approach on the bridge with the station in the window. Sometimes the dockmaster calls first; otherwise HAIL opens comms. Then DOCK. Some stations charge a small parking fee, and DOCK says so.

Once docked, your crew's active skills recharge. Market Row is always there; sometimes there is also someone in another room (a cantina, a clinic, an office) with something to say. Each room plays once per visit. When you LEAVE STATION you are back on the hub, stopped at the station, and the Stop menu now includes the station services you can pay for: MEDBAY, DRYDOCK and FAB SERVICE (each only shown when you need it), plus TRADE to go back to the market.

9 · Arriving

Reach the last stop and the voyage is over. The arrival screen shows the colony with your crop, under a title that tells you how well it went. VIEW REPORT opens the report card.

The report card shows:

  • the tier title, an arrival line about your crop at its new home, and a closing quote;
  • the Run Report: how many of the people who launched are still aboard, how full your fuel, food, water, hull and morale were, the day you arrived against the deadline (days early, on time, or days late), and your score, with NEW BEST when it beats your record for that destination;
  • score extras, when you earned them (soil surveys, a crop bonus);
  • READ THE COLONY REPORT, which unfolds the full ending text and the Ship's Record, the most notable things you did on the way;
  • NEW RUN, and back to the title.

A run that ends early (see How a run can end) gets its own screen with an epitaph and the Ship's Record instead.

The AI's speech bubble

While you are stopped the Stop menu covers the log column, so your AI talks to you in a speech bubble over the ship instead: its portrait and its newest three lines for this stop (tips, comments, warnings, memories of earlier choices). Click the bubble or its × to dismiss it; RESUME clears it too. Every line is also kept in the log and the Ship's Log, so nothing is lost. You can see the bubble in the walkthrough's hub screenshot.

The Stop menu

The Stop menu lists the jobs this place allows. Its header names the kind of place (DEEP SPACE, ASTEROID FIELD, NEBULA, STATION APPROACH…) with a line of mood, and a LOG button that opens the Ship's Log. Each job row shows its effort pips, how long it takes, and a one-line description. Hover for the details, including the morale the job will cost each crew member. A locked job says what it needs. Jobs with nothing to work on (REPAIR with a full hull, MEDBAY with nobody hurt) are not listed at all.

Events and choices

Every stop has one event. Some are quick decisions, some have several rounds of choices, some put you face to face with someone on a screen or in a corridor, and some turn into minigames or fights. The choices column is where the decisions are made, and it tells you a lot if you know how to read it.

  • The sub-label is the small line under a choice. It is always one line, and the full story is in the tooltip. It may say what the choice needs, preview what it does, or describe the job.
  • Effort pips ●●○○○ show how much work a choice is, from one to five. Every pip costs morale: each crew member pays the effort tax when you pick it, and more pips cost more morale, climbing faster than the pips do. The cost is in the tooltip, on Stop-menu jobs and event choices alike. With no crew aboard there is nobody to tax.
  • Effort checks. Hands-on jobs show Effort 65 / 40 · 2 days · 90% clean: the effort you can bring against the effort the job needs, the time it takes, and the chance it goes cleanly. Your own hands, every crew member, the right specialist and the right tool all add effort. A one-use tool (a repair kit, say) is spent when it counts. Short of effort, the job still gets done, just worse: the tooltip spells out exactly what "worse" costs. Even a well-equipped crew is never certain.
  • Locked choices show a padlock and Need: …: a crew role, an item, an amount of gold, or several at once. A crew member who is too badly hurt cannot help, and the lock says so. Some choices stay hidden entirely until something earlier in the voyage opens them.
  • Gate icons. An unlocked choice that uses something you have shows its icon: the crew member's portrait, the item, the gold.
  • Previews. Some choices show what they will do before you pick them. Your AI might (MARV flags the most expensive option), a clean SCAN does, some items do, and an Academic captain gets one research preview per station leg.
  • Risky choices have red sub-labels: they can go badly.
  • Tooltips. Hover any choice for the full picture: the days it takes, everything it costs, the effort and its morale tax, the odds, and what happens if you fall short.
  • Leaving. Many layered events have a red leave row at the bottom (LEAVE, NOT NOW, BACK TO DOCK…). It ends the scene with no outcome, unless its own sub-label or tooltip says otherwise: some leave rows are an exit with a price (backing off from a fight is never free). A few emergencies cannot be left, and their leave row is locked.

Many choices also take time. A quick decision is a day; real work can be several days; a few big undertakings take a couple of weeks. The tooltip always says.

ORDERS

Your standing orders apply every day until you change them. Click the orders strip under the ship (or a readout in it) to open ORDERS. Each row shows a projection that updates as you try options; APPLY keeps the change, CANCEL throws it away.

  • ENGINES sets the pace. LIGHT SPEED: crawl, burn little, the crew rests. RIDICULOUS: the default. LUDICROUS: faster, burns harder, the crew tires. PLAID: fastest, bleeds fuel and crew, and running at Plaid leg after leg heats the drive. The row projects your ARRIVAL day against the deadline, the day the fuel would run out, and the morale trend.
  • RATIONS: VENDING MACHINE (half rations: food lasts, morale sinks), STANDARD (morale holds), PIG OUT (extra: morale lifts, food empties fast). The row shows food per day and the day the food runs out.
  • WATER ALLOCATION splits the ship's water between the plant and the crew. Drag the slider. The marker shows what the crop needs; below it the crop is thirsty and slows, too far above it risks root rot, and starving the crew's side hurts morale and eventually health. The row projects when the plant matures and how the crew is doing.

The ARRIVAL projection is the same one the game uses to decide you are behind schedule. If it shows a ⚠ and a day past the deadline, you are on course to arrive late.

The cargo hold

The crate button in the header opens the hold. The top shows your fuel, food and water tanks with their level, daily use and the day each would run empty. Below are three tabs: GEAR (instruments, consumables, trade goods), MATERIALS and UPGRADES, with a SORT menu. The header counts slots used against capacity.

From the detail panel you can USE a consumable (a fuel cell, a ration brick, a crop treatment…), SELL it to the market while you are docked (no day spent), or JETTISON it to free a slot. Upgrades work just by being aboard.

The crew dossier

Click a crew card for their dossier: portrait, health and morale, a short bio, any status effects (wounded, morale crisis, deserting, going hungry or thirsty…), their PASSIVE skill (always on) and their ACTIVE skill with its uses and a USE button. Active skills recharge when you dock at a station. Arrows flip between crew members, and the captain has a shorter dossier of their own.

The growbay

Click the crop card to visit your crop. The growbay shows growth and health bars, the plant itself at its current stage, the stage name, the day the harvest will be ready, who is tending the plant, and every growth modifier in play. Under MONITORED are the threats that are not happening right now.

  • Water. Set the plant's share of the water. The plant's need is marked on the track.
  • Tender. Who looks after the plant. A botanist tends by default and is by far the best at it; anyone else helps a little. Click the tender card to change.
  • TREAT clears a pest outbreak. It takes a day of work unless a botanist handles it; the button's tooltip says which.
  • INSPECT opens the full read: vital signs, growth rate, species notes, the four stages and what can go wrong in each, and every active threat with its cause and fix.
  • HARVEST unlocks when growth reaches 100%. It gives food (zinnias give morale instead) and the plant starts a fresh cycle. Food that will not fit in the stores is pressed into ration bricks if the hold has room.

The fabricator

The fabricator turns materials into useful things. Open it from the Stop menu. Pick a recipe on the left to see what it makes, what it needs (with what you have), how many days it takes, how much wear it adds, the chance of a misfire and its morale cost. CRAFT starts the job.

  • Crafting takes real days, one at a time, while the fabricator is open, and the ship's usual daily drain applies. Close it and the job is held until you come back.
  • CANCEL before the first day finishes gives everything back; after that you get half the materials back.
  • Wear builds with every print and makes misfires more likely. At full wear the fabricator breaks. An engineer reduces wear, and a station's FAB SERVICE resets it.

The market and Market Row

At a station, TRADE (or the MARKET room when docked) takes you to Market Row, where a vendor runs a stall; out on the trail, TRADE hails a passing trader ship. BROWSE THE WARES opens the market.

  • BUY rows sell an item for gold. Station markets stock a wide catalog, including ship upgrades; a trader ship carries a smaller hold.
  • TRADE rows are barter: they take one of your goods instead of gold.
  • SELL pays gold for your items and materials. Trade goods each have their own value; a spectrometer aboard tells you exactly what it is before you sell.
  • Discounts (CHIP, a Merchant captain) apply to what you buy, never to what you sell.
  • STEP BACK closes the market. Browsing is free; leaving after you bought or sold costs a day.

The route map

The map button in the header shows the whole route: every stop, colored by type, from Departure to your destination, with YOU ARE HERE over the ship. Stops you have passed are marked visited. Click a stop to read what it is. Forks split into named lanes; until you pick, the lanes beyond them read ???. The map is for looking only: you choose lanes in the fork's event.

The Ship's Log

The LOG button in the Stop menu (or SHIP'S LOG in the pause terminal) opens the voyage's journal, written by your AI. It records every event you met and what you chose, what it really did to the ship, every arrival and what the leg cost, crop milestones, fights, and people joining or leaving. Entries with a link icon point to the real science behind a joke. Anyone lost on the voyage gets a memorial page.

The pause terminal

Press Esc any time for the Bridge Terminal. Time stops while it is open. It opens on a short ship status report.

  • RESUME closes the terminal.
  • SETTINGS: cursor color and outline, SHOW TIPS (the AI's first-run tips), and TEXT SPEED for the typewriter text.
  • SAVE and LOAD: three local save slots. You can save between encounters (not in the middle of an event or a job), and a loaded game returns to the hub. The game also autosaves as you play; CONTINUE on the title screen resumes it.
  • SHIP'S LOG opens the journal.
  • HELP lists the controls: click to act, hover to preview, drag sliders, Esc for this terminal or to close a window, 1–9 to pick a choice or a Stop-menu job, Tab and Enter to move between buttons and press one.
  • QUIT returns to the title screen after a confirmation. Anything since the last save or autosave is lost.

Your saves live in your browser, on this computer. Clearing the browser's site data clears them too.

Records

RECORDS on the title screen lists your best arrival per destination (score, tier, ship, crop, captain, day and crew) and your last 10 voyages, newest first: arrivals with their tier and score, and runs that ended early with how they ended. Only arrivals whose crop made it alive count as a best. CLEAR RECORDS wipes them, after asking.

What everything means

The seven resources

Fuel, food, water, oxygen and hull are ship supplies, each out of 100 (two upgrades raise the fuel and hull caps). Morale belongs to each crew member. Gold is money. The supply bars turn to warning colors when a supply runs low, and your AI will mention it.

Every run starts with 50 gold (more for some captains), a repair kit, a soil sampler, and a little metal and scrap. Mars starts short on food and water; Interstellar starts a little short on both, with a crew already less cheerful about the trip.

HP, wounds and morale

  • Health (HP) runs from 100 down. Below 40 a person is wounded: their skills work at half strength and their HP bar turns amber. At 25 or below it turns red, and they are too hurt to help with choices that need them. At zero they die. The captain has real health too.
  • Healing: everyone recovers a little each leg (faster with a medic aboard). The medic's Treatment, a medkit, and MEDBAY at a station heal properly.
  • Morale is per person. Changes to "the crew" hit everyone, but each specialist also reacts to their own concern: the botanist to the crop's health, the engineer to hull damage (and cheers up at a finished repair), the chef to short rations, the medic to injuries and deaths, the pilot to the pace, the xenobiologist to alien finds, the diplomat to fighting, and everyone to their own wounds. Hover a morale bar to see why it moved. With only one crew member aboard, those personal concerns count half.
  • Desertion. When someone's morale hits zero you are warned, and they will walk off at the next station unless their morale is back up to 25 by then. The morale bar and dossier say so.
  • Mutiny. If the crew's average morale reaches zero, they take the ship, and the run ends.
  • The captain has no morale. Flying solo (no crew at all) means no morale to manage and no effort tax, but also no specialists and fewer hands.
  • A vacant crew slot can sometimes be filled on the way, by someone you take aboard. The ship never carries more than captain plus two crew; anyone extra rides along as a passenger.

Captains

You are the captain, and your background is what you studied before this. It stands in for a missing specialist at half strength: half their passive effect and half their effort contribution. If you also hire that specialist, the better of the two applies; they do not stack. Some backgrounds also bring an item or extra gold.

Crops and how they grow

Your crop grows from 0% to 100% through four stages: Sprouting, Vegetative, Flowering and Maturing. At 100% you can harvest it, and it starts again from a fresh planting. Its health is what matters for the mission: it must be above zero when you arrive.

  • Growth is fastest when the plant gets the water it needs and someone good tends it. Being thirsty, pests, and the wrong surroundings slow it down.
  • Health slowly recovers with good watering and falls with thirst, pests and stress. Ordinary imperfect care mostly stalls it; real neglect kills.
  • Surroundings matter. Deep void runs cold, anomalies run hot, nebulae and the void are dim. The growbay shows these as modifiers while you are there; they pass when you move on.
  • Pests turn up now and then once the plant is established. TREAT them in the growbay or use a crop treatment.
  • A healthy crop helps the air: it makes some of the oxygen the crew breathes.

Items and materials

  • Items are things: instruments (a gas analyzer, a geiger counter, a portable drill…), consumables (fuel cells, ration bricks, water and O2 canisters, medkits, hull patches, crop treatments), trade goods worth gold, curiosities, and upgrades (a bigger fuel tank, hull plating, a shield module…). Gear takes a cargo slot each; small consumables stack several to a slot; upgrades take no slot and work as long as they are aboard. Many items open choices in events; the hold's detail panel lists where an item is used.
  • Materials are raw stock: metal, scrap, minerals, exotic matter and biocomponents. They come from mining, salvage, fights and events, and go into REPAIR and the fabricator, or get sold. Every 10 of a material takes one cargo slot.
  • The hold has limits. Capacity depends on the trail (and one upgrade adds more). A full hold means new finds stay behind: briefings warn you before you spend the time.

Deadlines and arriving late

Each trail has a deadline: the day the colony expects you. The day counter shows the days left, and ORDERS projects your arrival day at the pace you have set. If that projection drifts past the deadline, you are behind schedule, and the trail may let you know.

Being late never ends a run. When the deadline passes, your AI says so once and the ship flies on. You still arrive and still get your ending; the day counter switches to counting the days late, the report says how many, and every late day comes off your score. Arriving early adds to it.

How a run can end

Arrival. Reach the destination and the run is scored. The score weighs how much of your crew made it, how well-stocked the ship is (fuel, food, water, hull and morale), and how early or late you are, plus any extras such as soil surveys and a crop bonus. The score sorts the arrival into one of four tiers, from a triumphant MADE IT down to an arrival nobody is quite sure about, each with its own endings to discover. Each trail has its own scale, so a good Interstellar arrival is judged against the Interstellar trail. And if the crop is dead when you arrive, none of that matters: the crew made it, the mission did not, and the game will let you sit with that.

Ending early. A run can also end on the way:

  • the fuel runs out and the ship is adrift;
  • the hull fails;
  • the captain dies (whether or not anyone else is left aboard);
  • the crew's average morale hits zero and they mutiny;
  • too many days with no food, no water or no oxygen;
  • the crop dies.

Some of these leave a door open. When the crop dies you can choose to keep flying, though your arrival can then only be the hollow kind. And a certain item can give a stranded ship one last chance. If the whole crew is lost but the captain lives, the captain flies on alone.

Tips for surviving

Every trail

  • The crop comes first. Check the crop card every stop. Keep its water share at or just above what it needs, TREAT pests promptly, and harvest when it is ready: the food (or the morale) is yours, and the plant starts again.
  • Read the tooltips. Every cost the game knows about is on a tooltip before you commit: days, supplies, morale, odds, and what happens if you fall short.
  • Mind the pips. Heavy jobs tax every crew member's morale. A few big jobs in a row can leave the crew miserable even when everything went well.
  • Dock at stations. Docking recharges active skills, stations have the biggest market, and only stations will heal your crew or patch your hull for gold. Waving off is free in time but expensive in options.
  • Salvage pays. Derelicts and wreckage are where steady gold and materials come from, and gold buys medbays, drydocks and upgrades.
  • Use your specialists' active skills. They recharge at the next station, so an unused one is wasted.
  • Watch the ORDERS projection. It tells you the day you will arrive and the day each supply runs out at your current settings.
  • SCAN before a fork when you can spare the day.
  • Do not burn Plaid forever. Fast is tempting; the engines, the fuel and the crew all pay for it.

Lunar: the gentle trail

  • Lunar forgives a lot. Use it to learn the screens, try the minigames and get to know a crew.
  • Stations are plentiful, so spend gold freely on repairs and healing.
  • With a short trail, every lost day is a bigger share of the deadline: do not rest more than you need to.

Mars: the thirsty trail

  • You leave short on water and food, and events here ask much more of both. Water is the supply most likely to bite.
  • Do not over-water the crop "to be safe": give it what it needs and keep the rest for the crew. Ice from mining asteroids and water canisters are worth the time.
  • A chef, sensible rations and regular harvests keep the larder going; a food crop pays for itself here.
  • Trouble gets worse as the trail goes on. Reach the back half of the trip with supplies in hand, not scraping by.

Interstellar: the long trail

  • It is a long way through stranger places: voids, anomalies, long stretches between stations. Plan for long gaps between resupplies.
  • The crew starts less cheerful. Keep morale healthy early; it is much harder to rebuild later.
  • The hardest events wait for the final stretch. Bank fuel, hull and spare consumables for the end.
  • The fabricator earns its keep here: keep some materials and service it at stations.
  • Fuel matters over this distance. A pilot, sensible engine settings and a stock of fuel cells keep you from drifting.

PART 2 · BEHIND THE CURTAIN

How the game works underneath: the moving parts, how they talk to each other, and why the ship does what it does.

Spoilers past this point. Part 2 opens the hood: how events are picked, how the score is worked out, what the crew is quietly keeping track of. If you'd rather discover all that the hard way (the traditional way, in space), close this part now and come back after a few voyages. Nothing here is required to play. It is, however, extremely satisfying to read after a run went sideways.

This part describes concepts, not code. It explains each system in plain words and draws the connections between them. Where an exact number matters to understanding something, it gets a mention. Otherwise you'll read "a little", "a lot" and "much more likely", which is how the ship's crew would describe it too.

The big picture

Trans-plant is one long-running story about one ship. Everything the game knows about that story lives in a single place, the run's state. Every other part either reads it, changes it, or both.

  • The content library is the game's data: trails, events, crew, captains, AI companions, crops, items, materials, recipes, endings, failures and a great many flavor lines. It is loaded once when the game starts and never changes during a run.
  • The map generator builds a fresh route for the chosen trail at launch, so no two voyages share a map.
  • The run's state is the voyage itself: supplies, crew, crop, cargo, the map and where you are on it, the day, earlier choices, and the log.
  • Screens show the state and turn your clicks into changes: the cruise hub and Stop menu, encounters, Orders, the growbay, the crew dossier, the cargo hold, the market, the fabricator, the map.
  • The event system decides what happens at each stop, runs the encounter you play through, and applies its outcome.
  • The ship's daily clock charges each day that passes: food, water and air drawn, morale drifting, the crop growing, the deadline getting closer.
  • Minigames and jobs are the hands-on work: scanning, mining, salvaging, foraging, repairs, medical emergencies and so on. Their result decides what lands.
  • Combat is the ship-to-ship fight some events can start. It keeps its own little scoreboard during the fight, then hands the net result back.
  • The failure check runs after anything changes, and ends the voyage the moment one of the ways to lose comes true.
  • Endings write up the voyage: an arrival tier and score, or a failure card.
  • Records remember finished voyages and each destination's best, separately from any save.
  • Saving snapshots the state at safe moments, so Continue and Load can bring the voyage back.
  • The AI companion you picked gives the whole thing a voice: event lines, the log, tips, warnings and a few real perks.

A run from start to finish

A voyage moves through a small number of states. Most of the time you are either stopped at a stop (the Stop menu open) or in an encounter. Flying a leg is instant: pressing RESUME pays the leg's cost and lands you at the next stop.

Setup and the departure

The setup wizard collects the trail, the AI, the captain's background, up to two crew, the crop and the ship's name (left blank, the ship draws one). LAUNCH turns those choices into a fresh state: the trail's starting supplies, everyone at full health, each crew member at the trail's starting morale, the captain's starting bonus, a small default loadout, and a freshly generated map. About a third of voyages also quietly load a stowaway, who stays hidden for now.

Then the departure plays: one launch event at Earth Orbital Station on day 0. Its Continue opens the hub, stopped at the station, with the Stop menu up, exactly like every later stop.

The loop: stop, resume, arrive

After every event the ship is stopped, and the Stop menu is already open. RESUME is what flies. Before it travels, it checks a few things:

A fork is a stop of its own. Arriving there plays a route-decision event, and the choice you make in it sets which branch you're on. From then on the map shows one way forward and RESUME takes it.

The end

Reaching the destination ends the voyage at once with an arrival ending. Anything that empties the ship of a way to keep going ends it with a failure. Two failures offer a way on: a dead crop (Keep flying) and an empty fuel tank when you carry an emergency beacon. Choosing either un-ends the voyage and plays the stop that the failure interrupted on the way in, so it can't be skipped for free.

Saving, Continue and Load

The game keeps one autosave (what Continue resumes) plus three manual slots in the pause menu (SAVE and LOAD). The autosave is written:

  • every time the hub is redrawn, which is every safe moment between actions;
  • when you enter an event, and at every step deeper into its layers;
  • the moment an outcome lands (so a reload can't offer the same choice again);
  • with a special marker the moment a job begins, a fight changes, or a failure that offers a way on appears.

A manual SAVE is refused in the middle of an encounter or a job; the pause menu says why. A voyage that ended for good clears the autosave. A finished voyage can't be resurrected.

A reload mid-fight is generous only if the fight was already decided. If you had already won (a kill, a surrender, their retreat, your jump), that result lands. If you were in the defeat beat, the defeat lands. Otherwise the game plays out ATTEMPT ESCAPE for you, with the enemy firing at the drive the whole way, which is never better than playing on.

What isn't saved

Only the moment-to-moment bits are left out: text halfway through typing, the AI's speech bubble, a minigame or fight in progress (replaced by the markers described above), a wait in progress (a reload calls it off), and settings such as text speed and tips, which belong to you rather than to the voyage. The art seed is saved, so a reload shows the same backdrops and the same faces.

How an event happens

Every stop gets at most one event of its own. What it is depends on where you are, how the voyage is going, and what you did earlier.

What plays when you arrive

Stations

A station stop is a little visit rather than a single scene. On arrival, any passengers step off (and the rescued ones' families pay up), and anyone who was warned at zero morale and hasn't recovered walks off, before anything else happens. Then:

The pool: which events can appear

An event is eligible at a stop only if every one of its conditions holds right now:

  • Stop type. Events are written for planets, stations, asteroid fields, derelicts, nebulae, the void, anomalies, or anywhere. Forks are special: only route-decision events can appear there, because the fork's event has to pick a branch.
  • Trail. Some events belong to one or two trails only, which gives each trail its own texture.
  • Planet biome. A scene written for one kind of world only fires on a planet of that kind (the same biome its art shows).
  • Situations. Many events need a situation to be true: low on a supply, low crew morale, the start, middle or end of the journey, running behind schedule, a strained drive, a sick or pest-ridden crop, a dead crop you kept flying with, someone injured, a death earlier in the voyage, flying solo, the few stops after a fight, a worn or broken fabricator, a full or empty hold, a hidden stowaway past the early part of the trip.
  • Story flags. A follow-up only joins the pool once the earlier choice that sets it up has happened.
  • What you carry. A delivery or payoff event only fires while the story object is still in your hold. Jettison it and the payoff never comes.
  • Who is aboard. A scene where a crew member speaks needs someone who can say it; a scene about a particular role needs that role aboard as crew; a crop story needs a living crop.
  • Once only. Some events happen once per voyage. The departure and the halfway moment are one-of-a-kind too.

Difficulty shifts later in a trail

Every event is tagged easy, medium, hard or ultra. Each trail has a difficulty table for its early stops. Past a point in the journey, the table slides steadily toward a harsher one by the time you reach the destination. The shift only changes the odds; it never adds or removes events. On the Interstellar trail, the ultra tier shows up only on the late legs.

Repeat cooldown and families

An event that can repeat sits out for the next few stops after it fires. Some events belong to a family, such as the traders or the pirates. A family cools down as one, so meeting one pirate crew keeps all the pirates away for a while. Within a family, which face you meet at a stop is fixed for that stop, so TRADE and a WAIT at the same stop bring the same trader. If every eligible event is cooling down, the stop still gets one, the one drawn longest ago, rather than going empty. Events locked in by a SCAN count as drawn for this purpose, so a scan can't line up the same scene twice in a row.

The hard-streak relief

The game counts hard (and ultra) events in a row. After a run of several, it eases off: the next couple of draws lean heavily toward easy and medium, and the late-trail shift is suspended for them. It is a pity timer for death spirals. A SCAN's look ahead doesn't count toward the streak.

Situation events

When a situation is active (low water, a sick crop, an injury), the events written for it get a large boost. They become likely, not guaranteed, so ordinary content still turns up during a crisis. A situation's events share one boost between them, so a well-stocked situation gives you variety, not more crisis scenes. The positional situations (start and end of the journey) only get a mild bump. Branch labels add their own lean on top: a hostile branch favors fights, a scientific one favors science, a safe one favors easy and peaceful scenes. More on labels in The map.

Inside an event

Simple events are one screen: a body of text and a few choices, each leading to an outcome. Bigger ones are built from layers, like rooms you walk between. Each layer has its own narration, a line from your AI, maybe a line from the person you're talking to, and its own choices. Choices can lead deeper, go back, or end the event with an outcome.

The person across from you is rolled when the event starts (species, quirks, a name) and remembered, so revisits and reloads meet the same face. Many events also have alternate openings, rotated through the voyage, so a repeat reads differently.

Gates and locks

A choice can need a crew role, an item, gold, an earlier story flag, a living (or ailing) crop, a working fabricator, or room in the hold for what it grants. A locked choice stays visible and lists everything you're missing ("Need: Diplomat + 20g"). Story-gated choices just say so. A role you have can still be unable to help: someone hurt too badly can't answer a crew-gated choice, and the patient in a wound crisis can't treat themselves.

The one stand-in: a captain trained as a xenobiologist can take a xenobiologist's choice when there isn't one aboard, at half the gains.

Previews

Every choice's tooltip tells everyone how long it takes and what it costs. Beyond that, some choices show a one-line preview of what they'll do:

  • Posted terms. Some choices simply say what they do, such as a station botanist's advice.
  • MARV's read. With MARV aboard, the costliest choice on screen shows its costs. ("The cost of accuracy is knowing how bad it is.")
  • A perfect SCAN. At a stop you scanned perfectly, every choice shows its outcome.
  • Instruments. A medical scanner shows every choice's outcome in a wound crisis; a Geiger counter flags every choice that can hurt the crew.
  • The Academic captain's research. One choice's full outcome, once per station leg.

Previews are worked out by the same rules the outcome uses, so a preview never promises a number the ship would change (an engineer's better repairs, a recycler's saved air, the trail's supply bite).

Effort checks and pips

Many choices carry effort pips (●●●○○). Each pip level costs every crew member some morale the moment you commit, and the cost climbs steeply as the pips go up. With no crew aboard, there is no morale to tax.

Hands-on jobs (a wrench job, a calibration, a negotiation that might wobble) also have an effort check. The effort you bring is the captain's own hands, plus each listed specialist at their full value (half if wounded, half for a captain of that background), plus general help from everyone else, plus the right tool from the hold. The button shows the odds. Short of effort, you get a costlier version: thinner gains, extra days, and the job's own setback (or, for some jobs, a different outcome written for the botched attempt). Even a perfect crew keeps a little risk. Some repair jobs also scale their time by effort: a crew that can't contribute much takes up to twice as long, and a strong one finishes faster. A one-use kit is spent only when it actually changes the result.

What an outcome can change

An outcome can move any supply or gold (as a fixed amount or a share of what you have), morale (for the whole crew or one person), crop health, items and materials (as far as the hold has room), fabricator wear or repair, pests, standing orders, flags, and people: an injury, a bad wound, a death risk, healing, someone joining, a stowaway's fate. It also takes time, from no time at all up to a couple of weeks, and that time passes through the daily clock like any other day.

Severity: injury, bad wound, death risk

Harm to people comes in three grades. An injury drops someone below the wound line. A bad wound does the same and stays open: unless it's treated before the next station, a wound crisis fires there. A death risk kills, unless a medic's training turns it into a bad injury (a crew medic often, a medic captain less often, neither can save themselves), or, failing that, a carried medkit sometimes does, and is used up.

Flags that come back

Choices leave flags behind, and flags echo. The diagram shows the four ways one decision can return.

The AI's callbacks pick the weightiest memory that's waiting, at most one every other stop, and one is guaranteed after a few quiet legs. At the end, the Ship's Record lists up to five of the voyage's most notable choices as epilogue lines, in the order they happened.

Minigames from events

Some choices launch a minigame: a medical emergency, bracing against a solar surge, sealing a hull breach, a scan, an engine sequence, salvage or foraging. The event writes its own reward for each result (excellent, adequate, insufficient), and that is exactly what lands; the minigame's briefing and result card show those rewards, not a free-play haul. A medical emergency plays on a real patient (the worst hurt, the captain included) at their real HP, and the result lands on them. See Minigames and the Stop menu for how quitting works.

The ship's daily clock

Time only moves when something spends it, and every way of spending it goes through the same daily clock: a leg's flight, an outcome's time cost, a Stop-menu job, each day of REST or WAIT, treating the crop, each day of a fabricator print. So the same rules apply whether a day is spent flying or fixing.

Food, water and air

  • Food depends on rations (VENDING MACHINE eats half, PIG OUT half again as much), on mouths to feed (the captain, each crew member, any passengers; the engineer eats for two), and is trimmed by a chef aboard and an insulated cargo bay.
  • Air is a slow draw set by the trail. An O2 recycler halves it, and a healthy crop makes some of the air itself.
  • Water is one tank that both crop and crew drink from, at the trail's rate. The water split in Orders decides how it's shared. A botanist or a water purifier saves some of the crop's share, and the tank feels that saving.

The water split, both sides

The split is one value shown in two places (Orders and the growbay). The crop's side feeds the crop model: well under the crop's need slows growth, far under it hurts health, far over it rots the roots. The crew's side compares the crew's share with what the crop's need leaves them: the further short, the more morale it costs each day, and at the driest end people also lose a little HP each day. The split can't be pushed all the way to either side.

Morale drift

Every crew member's morale moves together with the group changes: rations (short rations sag, a pig-out lifts), a hard burn on flying days (LUDICROUS and PLAID tire people; LIGHT SPEED rests them), and the crew's water share. Once per leg, ARIA adds a little cheer, a chef who has food to cook adds a little more, and a healthy tomato or zinnia crop lifts spirits (sick zinnias drag them down). Then the role concerns add personal reactions on top (see The crew).

A leg

Flying a leg does a few things that a plain day doesn't:

Each trail has a travel-day budget and a fuel budget, spread evenly over the legs you'll actually fly (a fork's detour included). Pace scales both: faster means fewer days and more fuel. PLAID also builds up drive heat: each PLAID leg in a row makes a strain more likely (the chance doubles each time), a slower leg cools it a step, and LIGHT SPEED rests it fully. A strain means the next stop draws an engine-strain event.

Wear

The hull doesn't wear out on its own; it only loses points to events, fights and the odd drive-coil misfire. The fabricator wears with every print and breaks at full wear (it misfires more as it wears).

When something runs out

A day at zero food, water or air hurts everyone aboard, the captain included, and drains morale. Each resource keeps a count of days in a row at zero, and enough of them in a row is a failure of its own: starvation, dehydration or suffocation. Air runs out fastest, food slowest. There's a grace rule: the span in which a resource first runs dry can never by itself reach the failure, so you always get at least one stop to react. The count resets the moment the resource is above zero again.

The deadline and the behind-schedule warning

Each trail has a deadline, a little longer than a careful run takes. Passing it never ends a voyage: the AI says so once, the ship keeps flying, and the score pays for every day late.

The game keeps one arrival projection: days so far, plus the legs still to fly by the shortest route, each at the current pace plus an allowance for a typical stop. Orders shows it as ARRIVAL. Once a quarter of the trail is behind you, if that projection lands past the deadline, the behind-schedule situation turns on and its events can fire. Because both read the same projection, the warning fires exactly when Orders shows a late arrival.

The crew

The ship carries the captain and at most two crew. Each crew member holds a role (botanist, engineer, medic, pilot, chef, xenobiologist, diplomat) with a passive skill that is always on and an active skill used from the dossier, which recharges when you dock at a station.

Health

Everyone aboard has HP, the captain included. Below the wound line someone is wounded: their active skill is offline and their passive works at half strength (a wounded captain's own hands count half on hands-on jobs). Further down they're too hurt to help with crew-gated choices. At zero they die. Everyone heals a little each leg, more with a medic aboard and more again for the wounded with a medical scanner. The medic's Treatment, MEDBAY at a station, medkits and healing outcomes do the rest. A medic aboard also softens the direct damage people take, from poisoning, thirst, going without, or a fight.

Specialists and the captain

The captain has a background that matches one of the roles (or merchant, academic, veteran). It brings a starting bonus and that role's passive at half strength. A captain and a crew member of the same role don't stack: the better of the two counts. The captain can't stand in for a missing crew member on crew-gated choices, with the xenobiologist exception mentioned earlier.

Morale

Morale is per crew member; the captain has none. Group changes (outcomes, REST, rations, pace, water, deprivation, the effort tax, fights) hit everyone. A few outcomes name one person: the speaker, a role, or whoever is lowest.

On top of that, each role minds its own part of the ship, and only the person in that role feels it:

  • the botanist: the crop losing or regaining health, and a harvest;
  • the engineer: hull damage, and a finished repair;
  • the medic: anyone hurt, and anyone lost;
  • the pilot: days at a crawl (and enjoys a fast burn);
  • the chef: short rations, and an empty galley;
  • the diplomat: any fight at all;
  • the xenobiologist: alien finds;
  • everyone: being wounded themselves.

This is what lets one person drift away from the group. A lone crew member's concerns count half, so the whole crew's mood doesn't swing on one person's worries. The crew strip's MO bar tooltip says what last moved them.

Effort

Crew are also hands. On an effort check, a listed specialist contributes their full skill (half if wounded), everyone else adds general help, and the captain's own hands always count. That's why a two-person crew with the right roles finishes jobs a solo captain struggles with.

Deaths and leaving

Whatever road a crew member leaves by (death, the airlock, a dismissal, desertion, stepping down for a stowaway), they take everything that was theirs: the role, HP, skill charges, an open wound, morale, and any skill they had armed but not spent. A death is written into the Ship's Log as a memorial and sets a lasting flag. The voyage goes on as long as the captain lives. If the captain dies, it ends: captain lost with anyone else still aboard, or the autopilot ending if nobody is.

Stowaways and passengers

A hidden stowaway is revealed by an event once the trip is past its early part. You can welcome them (they join as crew in their own role if there's room and nobody holds it, otherwise they ride as a passenger), make room by having someone step down to ride as a passenger to the next station, or refuse. Rescued survivors work the same way: crew if there's room, otherwise a passenger who steps off at the next station, where their family pays a reward. A joined stowaway or survivor keeps their own name. Passengers eat like anyone else.

The crop

The crop is the mission. It lives in the growbay (not in cargo) and has two numbers: growth and health.

Growth and health, day by day

Each day the crop grows toward maturity at a rate set by its own maturity time, multiplied by its health and by every active modifier. Health moves by the sum of the modifiers' health effects. Every modifier the growbay lists is real and carries the numbers the day uses:

  • Water: matched to the crop's need it slowly heals; under it growth stalls and, badly under, health falls; far over it rots the roots; a dry tank hurts it most.
  • Temperature: the stop's environment (the void runs cold, anomalies hot) against the crop's ideal band.
  • Light: nebulae and the void are dim, which slows light-hungry crops.
  • Tending: whoever tends the bay adds growth (a botanist most, and a bonus to the yield).
  • Talking to it: after a station botanist's advice, a small lasting growth bonus.
  • Pests: slow growth and steadily eat health until treated.

Each crop has its own weaknesses and strengths that scale these (fragile zinnias, water-sensitive tomatoes, slow-recovering sweet potatoes, wheat's vulnerability to blight, soybeans that recover faster after pests). Tomatoes are also drama-prone: now and then a leg brings a small growbay incident that nudges their health or the crew's mood either way.

Sickness, pests and treatment

Below its sick line, a crop is sick: the AI says so, crop-sickness events join the pool, and the botanist frets. Pests arrive by a small daily chance once the plant is established. They can be cleared by TREAT in the growbay (a day of work, which a botanist spares you), a crop treatment from the hold, or an event outcome. The botanist's Greenhouse Protocol restores some health directly, and a botanist makes crop recovery from events go further.

Harvest

At full growth the crop is ready. Harvest (from the growbay or the crop card) pays its yield: food for most crops (anything past a full store is packed into ration bricks, if there's room), morale for zinnias. The bay replants at once and starts a new cycle.

The crop dying

If health reaches zero, the crop-dead failure appears. It offers Keep flying. Accepting turns on a lasting "crop dead" situation, so a few aftermath events can appear, and the arrival will be hollow no matter how well the rest went.

The map

Each trail has a fixed length, a number of forks, and tables saying which kinds of stops are likely early on and later. At launch the route is generated fresh.

Forks and branch labels

Each fork has two branches, and each branch gets a label: hostile, lucrative, safe, corporate, derelict-heavy, scientific or agricultural. About a third of forks hide one branch's label ("???"); a hidden label still shapes its branch. Labels are real in two ways:

  • They lean the branch's stop types (a derelict-heavy lane fills with derelicts, a corporate one with stations, an agricultural one with planets), and every labeled branch is guaranteed at least one of its signature stops, so a label never lies.
  • They lean the events on every stop of the branch: hostile toward fights and hard scenes, lucrative toward rewards, safe toward easy and peaceful ones, scientific toward science, agricultural toward crop and food, corporate toward stations and traders.

Scanning ahead

SCAN draws the events waiting at the stops ahead now and locks them in, so what the scan reports is what you meet. A good scan reads the next stop (every branch of a fork); a perfect one reads two stops deep and also turns on outcome previews for every choice at those stops; a poor one reads only static. The report names each event and how hard it is. A locked-in event is checked again on arrival: if it can no longer happen (you sold the thing it needed, say), the stop draws afresh. The ship also announces some stop types as you approach (good mining ahead, a derelict, an unidentified signal).

Minigames and the Stop menu

The Stop menu offers verbs that depend on the kind of stop: MINE in asteroid fields, SALVAGE at wrecks, FORAGE (or GATHER, HUNT, SCAVENGE) on planets and rocks, CONDENSE in nebulae with the right upgrade, SCAN, REPAIR, REST, the FABRICATOR, and the station services. TRADE appears when a trader is in range (every station has one; elsewhere each stop rolls once whether one is around, likelier near civilization); otherwise WAIT FOR TRADER takes its place. A verb with nothing to work on, like REPAIR on an intact hull, isn't offered at all.

How a job works

  • The effort tax is taken when you click, but held: closing the briefing (or leaving the mining field before your first run, or walking out of the engine room untouched) hands it back.
  • BEGIN is the point of no return. The game marks the save, so a reload from here is a quit.
  • Quitting keeps nothing, not even what was already in hand, and still spends the time. Harm already taken (a poisoning while foraging) stays taken.
  • The result card grades the run (excellent, adequate or insufficient) and lists what lands, as it will really land: a full food store or hold is shown as left behind.

Most jobs take a day. MINE takes a day per run. REST and WAIT pick a number of days and tick through them one at a time, with the full daily clock each day; waiting outside a station isn't safe: each day can bring a pirate, a distress call or an anomaly, which ends the wait and opens that event (a broken rest keeps the morale for the days it got). Station services (MEDBAY, DRYDOCK, FAB SERVICE) cost gold, not days. The fabricator charges per print and runs its days only while you watch it; closing it holds the job.

Minigames launched from events

Combat

A fight starts from an event choice that opens fire, or from a ram that doesn't get through (ramming works most of the time; when it doesn't, you take a knock and the fight starts anyway). Fleeing resolves straight to its own outcome. Every fight is fought from the bridge: their ship in the window, their captain on the feed. The fight tracks its own hull and crew-morale numbers, then hands the net result back to the ship.

Your options

  • Fire: click their ship to aim. A shaky crew can miss; a keen one lands more critical hits; a xenobiologist who identifies the enemy adds damage.
  • Evasive maneuvers: soften the next hit (less so with a frightened crew); a pilot adds a chance to dodge it entirely.
  • Hull patch (engineer, once per fight, below half hull) and boost shields (with a shield module, once per fight, blocks the next hit) are free actions.
  • Offer a tribute on the opening turn: the botanist's harvest or the chef's cookies, each a gamble costing food either way. Pirates who took the cookies remember them, and let you pass if you meet them again before the next station.
  • Attempt escape: the drive spools for a few turns (fewer with a pilot) while they keep firing, though the spool makes you harder to hit. It costs fuel when it jumps.

Their side

Pirate ships vary by species: some are tougher, and some never surrender. On longer trails they hit harder and take more punishment. The diplomat softens their opening shot. A badly damaged pirate of a surrendering species usually offers terms, or sometimes just flees. If the crew's morale collapses mid-fight, they stop firing, and the only order left is to stand down. With REX aboard, the first hit of every fight lands on point-defense instead of the hull.

How fights end

  • Destroyed: salvage the wreck (full loot and a bonus roll, more days) or leave it (partial loot, back sooner).
  • Surrender accepted: part of the loot, a morale lift. Finished them: the full loot, a morale hit, and a flag the galaxy remembers.
  • Tribute taken, escaped, or they fled: no loot, the fight's costs only.
  • Stood down: they take a toll of fuel and gold (a species that won't accept a yield treats it as a defeat).
  • Defeated: the ship limps away with hull barely holding, a share of the cargo is taken, and people may be hurt or, rarely, killed (a medic and high morale make that rarer).

Whatever happened, the diplomat minds the fight, the engineer minds each hull hit, and the few stops afterward count as a post-combat window for aftermath events.

How a run is scored and ended

Reaching the destination ends the voyage with an arrival. Its tier comes from a score worked out at that moment, from the crew who made it, what's left aboard, and the time.

  • The crop is the gate: a dead crop makes the arrival hollow whatever the numbers say. A living crop multiplies the score by its colony value (tomatoes only count if they arrive in decent health); living zinnias add nothing to the score but change the ending's final paragraph. The crop's condition (thriving or sick) also changes the ending's text.
  • Crew is the share of the crew you launched with who are still aboard (a captain who chose to fly solo gets half credit).
  • Supplies are the average of what's left, crew morale included.
  • Time is the share of the deadline left in hand. It is weighted heavily, and the same rate runs backward once you're late, so every late day costs as much as an early day earns. Late arrivals also get endings that don't claim you were on time.

Each trail draws its own bracket lines, calibrated so careful runs spread across the tiers by how well they went on that trail. The ending text is then assembled from the tier, the crop's condition, how the crew fared, the destination, and the Ship's Record of your notable choices.

Failures

The emergency beacon is one use: someone answers, and who it is is a roll. A friendly hauler refuels you for nothing, a corporate tug refuels you and sends an invoice, and the wrong people refuel you and then help themselves to your gold and a crate. Keep flying after a dead crop carries on toward a hollow arrival. Either way, the stop the failure interrupted still plays.

Records and bests

A voyage goes into the records once its ending is final: at once for an arrival or a terminal failure, or, for a failure that offers a way on, when you accept it (by reading the report, or by launching a new voyage over its save). The records keep the last ten voyages, newest first, and each destination's best arrival. Only arrivals with a living crop count for a best, a higher score wins, and on a tie the faster voyage does. Beating the best shows NEW BEST on the report. Records live apart from saves, so starting over never clears them.

The AI companions

The AI you pick at setup is the voice of the whole voyage. Almost every line the ship says passes through it.

  • Events carry a line written for each AI, so the same scene reads five ways.
  • The Ship's Log records choices, outcomes, arrivals, fights and deaths in the AI's own register.
  • Tips come one per new stop, the most urgent first (pests, a wound, low supplies, low morale), then an introduction to one system at a time. Each topic is said once per voyage, and the pause menu can switch them off.
  • Warnings fire when a supply or the crew's morale crosses its low line going down, and again at a critical line. Recovering and dipping again warns again.
  • The deadline passing is announced once, in the AI's voice.
